Speed to lead: why calling back in under a minute changes conversion

Adel Atya · 23 November 2026

Speed to lead means how long a new lead waits before a person or system reaches them. The research says the odds of reaching and qualifying a lead fall sharply in the first hour, so the first few minutes matter more than any script.

That claim comes with limits. The best-known data is old, and it covers web forms, not phone calls. Here is what it shows and what to do with it.

What does the research on speed to lead say?

The most quoted study is a 2007 analysis from InsideSales.com, presented with MIT's James Oldroyd. According to the study summary, it covered three years of data from six companies: over fifteen thousand web leads and over one hundred thousand call attempts.

The findings that matter here:

  • The odds of making contact with a lead fall by over 10 times in the first hour.
  • The odds of qualifying a lead (getting them willing to start the sales process) fall by over 6 times in the first hour.
  • Calling in 5 minutes instead of 30 cuts the odds of contact 100 times and the odds of qualifying 21 times.

The study's authors say response time mattered more than the day of the week or the time of day.

Why should you not take those numbers at face value?

First, the study was presented by the CEO of InsideSales.com, a company with a stake in fast calling, and it looked at web-generated leads called by sales reps. A homeowner with a leak is a different buyer. Treat it as a direction, not a forecast.

Second, it is from 2007, so habits may have changed.

Third, "100 times" is a ratio of odds, not a promise that your sales rise 100 times. The study itself says it did not look at close ratios, only contact and qualification.

A later Harvard Business Review piece by Oldroyd and two co-authors makes the softer point that most companies are not responding nearly fast enough. That part is safe to repeat.

Does this apply to a missed phone call?

Mostly yes, and for a plain reason: a person who called you wants something now. A web form can sit in a tab. A ringing phone is an active request.

Take a made-up example. A homeowner has water coming through a ceiling at 6pm. She calls the first plumber and gets voicemail.

She calls the second, who answers. She does not leave a message. The first plumber's callback at 7:15 reaches someone whose job is already booked.

How common is the first plumber's situation? Invoca's 2026 home services benchmark report found that 52% of callers to home services businesses speak with a person. Across sub-industries the range was 32% to 74%. Of the answered calls, 38% were leads, and 45% of those leads converted on the call.

That leaves the other 48% of callers, who did not speak with a person on that call. That is where speed to lead matters.

What is a realistic response time?

Here are the steps most small shops can actually reach, from slowest to fastest:

  1. Same day. Someone checks voicemail at lunch and at close. Slow.
  2. Within an hour. A person watches a shared inbox. It works until that person is on a roof or in a crawl space.
  3. Within a few minutes. An automatic text goes out at once, saying you saw the call and asking what they need.
  4. Within a minute. A text goes out, and a call comes back right away.
  5. Answered live. An agent picks up the call in about a second, so there is no callback at all.

In my view the biggest gap is between step 1 and step 3. That is my opinion from the work, not a measured result.

When is a one-minute callback not worth it?

Skip the automation, or at least wait, in these cases:

  • You get a handful of calls a week. If you can return each one yourself in ten minutes, a person is cheaper and warmer.
  • Your calls are mostly complicated. If every job needs an on-site look before anyone can quote, the first call is a conversation, not a booking.
  • Your callers are not in a hurry. A custom kitchen remodel is not an emergency. A callback at 9am next day may lose nothing.
  • You have no capacity. Calling back fast helps only if you can serve the job. If the trucks are booked for two weeks, speed just gives you a longer waiting list.

Speed also cannot rescue a bad offer. If your quote is far above the market, the fastest callback only gets you a quicker no.

  • Call your own business number from a mobile phone at 7pm and again on a Saturday. Note who answers and how long it takes.
  • Count last week's missed calls and how many got a reply within 5 minutes, within an hour, and the next day.
